BUT cannot be used for categorical features (in this case you can use most frequent value)

XGBoost
extreme gradient boosting (regression/classification)
fast and powerful
gradient descent to minime loss when adding new trees -> boosted group of decision trees
IT'S LIKE An ensable of trees (sum of predictions) BUT:
XGBoost is an ensemble of decision trees, but specifically it is an ensemble built using gradient boosting, not just a generic collection of trees.
Here’s the idea in simple terms:
Each individual model in XGBoost is usually a decision tree (typically shallow trees).
Trees are added sequentially, not independently.
Each new tree is trained to correct the errors made by the previous trees.
Imagine you’re trying to predict a number (regression).

1) First tree
Makes a rough prediction.

2) Compute errors
For each data point, calculate how wrong the prediction was.

3) Second tree
Is trained to predict those errors (not the original target).

Repeat

4) Final prediction

Add up predictions from all trees:
All trees are combined (summed) to produce the final prediction.
Y = e1(x) + e2(x) + ...

it's not made for sagemaker, it's opensource
Training input expected: 
	csv or libsvm
	recentrly aws updated recordIO-wrapped protobuf and parquet
A lot of hyperparameters, some of them:
	subsample: prevent overfitting
	eta: step size, prevents overfitting
	gamma,alpha(L1 reg.),beta(L2 reg.) -> larger = more conservative
	eval_metric: optimize error,AUC,...
	scale_pos_weight: unbalanced data
	max_depth: too high -> overfit
MEMORY-BOUND, NOT COMPUTE-BOUND!!

################# PLOTS #################

    Bar Chart → Examining the frequency of purchases for different items over a specific time period

    A bar chart is best for comparing counts across discrete categories.
    Here the categories are typically items (SKUs) (optionally grouped by a time window like day/week/month), and the measure is number of purchases.

    Histogram → Understanding the concentration and distribution of transaction values in a continuous range

    A histogram is specifically for continuous numeric variables (e.g., transaction value, basket value).
    It buckets values into ranges (bins) and shows how many transactions fall into each range, which directly reveals distribution, skew, and concentration.

    Heatmap → Identifying patterns in purchasing behavior across various time intervals and item categories

    A heatmap is ideal when you want to see patterns over two dimensions at once.
    Here the two dimensions are time intervals (e.g., hour-of-day/day-of-week) and item categories, and color intensity shows purchase volume or revenue, making trends/seasonality and hotspots easy to spot.

Note: a density plot can also represent the distribution of a continuous variable, but since you had to select three, the cleanest one-to-one mapping is Histogram for distributions, with Heatmap for two-dimensional patterns and Bar Chart for discrete frequencies
